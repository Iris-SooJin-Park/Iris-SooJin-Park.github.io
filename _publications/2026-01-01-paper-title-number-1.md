I show that household myopia and price perception rationalize puzzles in health care demand. Benchmark models of health care demand assumes forward-looking households that anticipate how current spending lowers future prices under nonlinear cost-sharing. I find evidence inconsistent with this model in the context of a modest cost-sharing introduction into a previously zero-deductible employer health plan. Aggregate spending responses are modest but increase steeply with health risk. A structurally estimated forward-looking model cannot rationalize this pattern, predicting little response among the high-risk households who anticipate reaching the out-of-pocket maximum, even though they reduce spending the most in the data. I show that this mismatch is explained by myopia and price-perception frictions. Myopic households may respond to the spot price of care, a pattern noted in prior work, but this price is often perceived imperfectly. Households learn prices once bills arrive, and often respond to average rather than marginal prices, a pattern I document using administrative billing data and survey experiment on explanation-of-benefits interpretation. High-risk enrollees face longer billing delays and are more likely to have claims that create a gap between average and marginal prices, generating a steep risk gradient in perceived prices. A behavioral model incorporating these frictions rationalizes the aggregate response and risk gradient. The results suggest that models of health care demand should account for perceived prices, not only contract incentives.
